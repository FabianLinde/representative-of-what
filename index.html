Representative of what?

The EU AI Act requires training data to be “sufficiently representative” without saying representative of what. Two measures answer that question differently.

  • The datasetHow the training data is split across four groups. Drag any bar to change it.
  • The even splitA flat line at 25% per group. The uniform measure asks how close the bars sit to this.
  • The actual populationThe stepped outline shows who the system will actually encounter: 55%, 25%, 15% and 5%. The empirical measure asks how close the bars sit to this.

Uniform representativeness

1.00

Shannon evenness. Reaches 1 when every group holds an equal share.

Empirical representativeness

0.70

One minus total variation distance. Reaches 1 when the dataset matches the population exactly.

Uniform
−∑g∈G pg ln pgln R
Empirical
1 − ½ ∑g∈G |pg − qg|

pg is the dataset’s share of group g, qg the population’s share of the same group, and R the number of groups. G is the set of all groups, so both sums run over every one of them. Only the second takes q as an input at all.

Drag any bar to reshape the dataset; both readings update.

Both readings run from 0 to 1, and on both, higher means more representative. They are measuring representativeness for the same dataset, but are pointed at different references: one at an even split across groups, the other at the actual, empirical population. The two measures would be equal in case the actual population would be evenly split across groups.

Each dataset scores 1.00 on one measure. A provider who computes an evenness index reports that A is the ideal dataset, while a provider who computes distance to the deployment population reports that B is. Both have measured representativeness, but based on different reference distributions.

Which reference is right depends on the deployment

These two systems both fall under Annex III of the AI Act, and both are therefore governed by the same data-governance obligation. The reference that serves one of them is wrong for the other.

Case-law retrieval for a judicial authority

Annex III, point 8(a)

The system reports what the law is. Its output is correct insofar as it reflects the body of sources as those sources actually stand. If decided cases are unevenly distributed across outcomes or subject matter, that unevenness is a fact about the law. Rebalancing the index toward an even split would make the system misdescribe the legal landscape, and a judge relying on it would form a distorted impression of how comparable matters have been decided.

The empirical reference is correct here. Evenness is not a virtue; it is a distortion.

Decision support in public administration

Annex III, point 5

The system produces assessments about individual people, and its purpose is not historical accuracy but accurate and fair decision-making. A group sparsely represented in the training data is one the model has had least opportunity to learn, so the system performs worst for exactly those applicants. Adopting the empirical distribution of applicants as the reference guarantees that the smallest groups receive the least reliable assessments, and it is the members of those groups who bear the consequences.

A uniform reference, or a deliberate departure from the empirical one, is defensible here.

What follows

A representativeness score without a declared reference cannot be interpreted. The same dataset yields different values, and ranks differently against alternatives, depending on a choice that no current instrument requires anyone to disclose. The choice is made regardless, since every measure presupposes one — but it is made silently, and usually by default, because the most accessible measures carry the even split built in.

The proposal is procedural rather than substantive. Providers should state the reference distribution against which representativeness was assessed, and justify it against the intended purpose. That does not make the choice correct. It makes it reviewable.
